# Founder Operating System

## Overview
Use this skill to design the founder's decision rhythm: what gets reviewed, how priorities are set, which metrics matter, and how chaos is prevented from eating the company. The goal is not ceremony. The goal is repeatable attention discipline.

## When to Use
Use this skill when the main problem is **how the company is run week to week and month to month**.

Typical triggers:
- founder priorities are unstable or unclear
- too many threads are competing for attention
- no consistent review rhythm exists
- decisions are getting made ad hoc and forgotten
- metrics exist but are not shaping action
- the founder needs an operating cadence that fits a small team

Do **not** use this as the main skill when:
- the task is org structure or reporting-line design → use `org-design-after-first-hires`
- the task is pure communication formatting → use communication or founder-update skills
- the task is narrow personal productivity with no company operating-system question

## Core Principle
A startup usually fails from decision drift and attention fragmentation before it fails from lack of ideas.

## Always cover
- current stage and bottleneck
- weekly priorities and stop-doing logic
- decision log or decision hygiene
- KPI review
- product / engineering / revenue sync
- risk register or escalation review
- monthly strategy review
- what the founder should stop carrying personally

## Workflow
1. Identify the current stage, bottleneck, and failure mode of the operating system.
2. Define the minimum cadence the founder can actually sustain.
3. Pick 3 to 5 metrics that matter now.
4. Create review rhythms: daily, weekly, monthly, and escalation-driven.
5. Define how priorities change, who owns follow-through, and when the system should be revised.

## Stage guidance
### Solo founder / very early team
- keep the system lightweight
- prioritize clarity of weekly goals and decision capture
- avoid process that exists only to mimic larger companies

### Small founding team
- tighten ownership, weekly reviews, and risk visibility
- keep strategy, product, and revenue loops linked
- make trade-offs explicit when priorities conflict

### Growing team
- add clearer operating rituals and delegation rules
- separate company-level review from function-level review
- reduce founder centrality where decision latency becomes a bottleneck

## Output format
Return:
1. current bottleneck diagnosis
2. weekly operating cadence
3. monthly review cadence
4. key metrics and decision rules
5. risk / escalation / reprioritization triggers

## Common pitfalls
1. **Tracking too many metrics.** A founder scorecard should focus attention, not diffuse it.
2. **Confusing activity with progress.** Busy calendars are not operating systems.
3. **Changing priorities too often.** Constant motion can look like energy while destroying execution.
4. **No explicit risk review.** Problems compound when nobody owns escalation.
5. **Overbuilding process before the company earns it.** Early systems should be light and sharp.
6. **Founder keeping all decisions by default.** That eventually turns speed into bottleneck.

## Verification checklist
- [ ] I designed a cadence the founder can actually sustain.
- [ ] I selected only stage-relevant metrics.
- [ ] I included reprioritization or escalation triggers.
- [ ] I tied the system back to actual execution quality.
- [ ] I kept the operating system proportionate to stage.

## Examples
### Example: chaotic early startup
Return:
1. Main issue is attention fragmentation, not missing strategy documents.
2. Set one weekly priority list, one decision log, and one risk review checkpoint.
3. Track only a few leading metrics tied to current bottleneck.
4. Add a monthly review for strategy, learnings, and stop-doing decisions.
5. Kill rituals that do not change decisions or execution.

### Example: founder bottleneck
Return:
1. Founder is acting as routing layer for too many decisions.
2. Keep high-stakes decisions founder-led, but delegate repeatable operational calls.
3. Create explicit weekly ownership review instead of ad hoc follow-ups.
4. Use escalation triggers for when work re-enters founder attention.
5. Revisit cadence when team size or complexity changes materially.
